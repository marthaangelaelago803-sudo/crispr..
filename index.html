01 / THE SCIENCE

Think of it as molecular scissors.

CRISPR originally evolved as part of bacterial defense against viruses. Scientists adapted the CRISPR–Cas9 system to make targeted changes to DNA.

CRISPR stands for clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats. The best-known editing system uses a guide RNA and a cutting enzyme called Cas9.

01 / LOCATE↗

Find the sequence.

A guide RNA helps direct the Cas9 protein toward a matching target in the DNA.

02 / CUT↗

Make a cut.

In classic CRISPR–Cas9 editing, Cas9 cuts DNA near the target. Other CRISPR tools can work differently.

03 / REPAIR↗

Let cells repair.

Natural repair mechanisms respond to the cut. They can create changes, but results are not always predictable.

A milestone in medicine: CASGEVY

The FDA first approved the CRISPR-based therapy CASGEVY in 2023 for sickle cell disease. It is also approved for transfusion-dependent beta-thalassemia. Approval details vary by indication and jurisdiction.

04 / A BREAKTHROUGH STORY

Decades in the making.

CRISPR didn't appear overnight. A series of discoveries transformed an unusual bacterial defense mechanism into a powerful tool for biology.

1987

A curious discovery

Researchers report unusual repeated DNA sequences in bacteria, an early clue to what would later be called CRISPR.

2012

A programmable tool

Emmanuelle Charpentier and Jennifer Doudna help demonstrate how CRISPR–Cas9 can be adapted for targeted DNA cutting.

2020

Nobel Prize in Chemistry

Charpentier and Doudna receive the Nobel Prize for developing a method for genome editing.

2023

A treatment milestone

The FDA grants its first approval for a CRISPR-based medicine, CASGEVY, for sickle cell disease.

05 / ETHICS & RESPONSIBILITY

With great power comes big questions.

Scientific possibility isn't the same as ethical permission. Genome editing requires careful oversight, transparency, and public discussion.

The potential

Carefully developed genome-editing approaches may offer meaningful benefits, but evidence is needed for each application.

  • Treating certain serious genetic diseases
  • Understanding how genes contribute to illness
  • Studying and improving crops

The responsibility

Editing DNA can have unintended effects. Some applications create especially serious questions about future generations and equity.

  • Safety and off-target changes
  • Access, fairness, and informed consent
  • Heritable edits and oversight
